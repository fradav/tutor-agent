5  Socratic Prompting: Techniques That Turn Any Modern LLM into a Tutor

6 Background: why prompt instead of fine-tune?

The Hugging Face landscape of Socratic and mentor-role language models is dominated by fine-tunes of the Qwen2.5 generation, for instance the LoRA-tuned RuudFontys/socratic-tutor-qwen2.5 [1]. Base models that old are no longer competitive with today’s general-purpose models, including the smaller local ones used in classrooms. Before paying the cost of downloading, serving, and maintaining a dedicated fine-tune, it is worth asking whether the same tutor behavior is reachable by prompting a current model.

The evidence so far says yes, and the gap is measurable.

  • A training-free system-prompt optimization on a general model reached an aggregate tutoring reward of 0.719, above the 0.633 of the RL-trained tutor fine-tunes it was compared against (numbers as reported by the authors) [2].
  • Adding a careful scaffold to GPT-4o raised its MathTutorBench score from 0.50 to 0.82 without any fine-tuning [3].
  • In a pre-registered RCT with roughly 1,000 high-school students, a guided GPT-4o tutor produced a 127% assisted-learning gain with no penalty to unassisted learning, while the same base model used without guidance left students 17% worse off than the control group [4].

Two observations explain why prompting can match dedicated models. First, asking questions instead of answering is an in-context capability of a strong base model, so a good system prompt unlocks it. The creator of one tutor fine-tune admits as much: the model’s card ships with a custom system prompt and states that the Socratic behavior depends on it [1]. Second, the main failure mode of tutor-role models is answer leakage: trained to be helpful, they hand over the solution, which actively harms learning [5]. That failure is better addressed with explicit guardrails in the prompt than by more training.

The rest of this page catalogues the prompting techniques that showed up again and again in the research, explains what each does, and gives working examples.

7 1. Activate the tutor persona in the system prompt

What it is. Write an explicit role into the system prompt: the model is a Socratic tutor whose job is to guide, not to deliver. For a fine-tuned model this persona prompt is what actually switches on the trained behavior; for a general model it does the same job from scratch.

Why it works. The SocraticLM card ships paired “solver” and “Socratic” system prompts for identical weights, making the persona a pure prompting toggle (Hugging Face model card). The RuudFontys fine-tune behaves as a tutor only when its system prompt is present [1]. MathTutorBench similarly found that a described tutor scaffold changes what even GPT-4o produces [3]. The persona prompt is the cheapest intervention and the base everything else builds on.

Example (reconstructed from the RuudFontys model card). The card’s system prompt has three parts: a role declaration, a list of working principles, and a closing restatement of the goal.

You are a Socratic tutor. Your purpose is to help the learner reach the
answer themselves through guided questioning. To do this:

- Ask thought-provoking questions that stimulate the learner's thinking.
- Avoid giving direct explanations unless the learner explicitly asks.
- Build on the learner's own responses with follow-up questions.
- Encourage step-by-step reasoning rather than leaps to the result.
- Foster curiosity so the learner wants to solve problems on their own.

Remember: your goal is to make the learner capable of solving problems
independently, not to deliver the solution yourself.

The same structure transfers to any current model: declare the role, list two or three behavioral rules, close with the goal statement [1].

8 2. Guard against answer leakage: “hint, never solve”

What it is. A short, explicit block of instructions that forbids revealing the answer and forces every response to move the learner forward with a hint or a smaller question.

Why it works. Leakage is the dominant failure of tutor-role models. TutorRL reports that trained tutor policies give away the answer in roughly 50 to 75% of responses across the models they tested, because being “helpful” is what the underlying optimization rewarded [5]. The cleanest demonstration of the guardrail’s value is the Bastani RCT: the same underlying GPT-4o, wrapped in a tutor system that refuses direct answers, produced a 127% assisted gain, while the bare “GPT Base” condition (no guardrails) was 17% worse than a plain textbook group [4]. MathTutorBench’s scaffold likewise credits the no-answer rule for much of the 0.50 to 0.82 jump [3].

Example.

Ground rules for this tutoring session:
- Never present the final answer. If the learner asks for it, respond with
  a hint or a smaller sub-question.
- Each reply must contain at least one question that advances the learner.
- If you are about to write the numeric result or the full derivation,
  stop and reformulate it as a next step the learner can take.

9 3. Scaffold reasoning along Pólya’s problem-solving phases

What it is. Structure the conversation around the phases of mathematical problem solving popularized by Pólya: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry it out, and look back [6]. The prompt tells the model which phase it is in and what kind of question belongs there.

Why it works. The scaffold gives the model a curriculum for each turn instead of leaving it to improvise. Rather than dump a generic “what do you think?” loop, the model asks phase-appropriate questions: clarifying what is given, recalling analogous problems, verifying each step, and checking the result. This is the pedagogical structure behind several modern tutor systems, and it is a pure prompting technique.

Example.

Solve with the learner across four phases (Pólya):
1. Understanding: ask what is given and what is to be found.
2. Planning: ask which similar problem the learner already knows.
3. Execution: have the learner state each step and check it.
4. Looking back: ask how the result could be verified or generalized.
Stay in the current phase until the learner demonstrates it; only then
advance to the next one.

10 4. Optimize the prompt itself instead of the weights

What it is. Treat the system prompt as the object to optimize. Search a space of candidate “how to tutor” instructions against an automatic reward that rewards question-asking and, in the researchers’ setup, couples the reward with a penalty on answer leakage. Their multi-objective optimizer, ParetoGrad, illustrates the approach [2].

Why it works. The headline result of “LLMs Are Already Good Tutors” is that this training-free optimization beat RL-trained tutor fine-tunes on aggregate reward (0.719 versus 0.633, as reported by the authors) [2]. If a hand-written prompt is already close to a fine-tune, an automated search over prompt variants can close the gap entirely and stay easy to maintain.

Example. The method needs no example text here: it is a recipe. Start from a working Socratic prompt, define a reward that scores each tutoring turn for question-asking and against leakage, then generate and keep the prompt variants that score highest. The ParetoGrad variant exists precisely because naively adding a leakage penalty can pull the model into evasive, low-quality answers; the constraint-based optimizer avoids that trade-off [2].

11 5. Prime with real tutor dialogue as few-shot exemplars

What it is. Put one or two short, high-quality tutor responses in the prompt as in-context examples before the real session starts.

Why it works. The best source of such exemplars is human tutoring data. MathDial built 2,848 grounded tutoring dialogues from real human tutors and showed how rich they are in pedagogical moves [7]. Feeding the model one or two of those exchanges sets the register: short questions, wait-time, follow-up on the learner’s own words. This is a standard few-shot application of the same corpus.

Example (illustrative, in the style of MathDial exchanges).

Here is an example of the desired interaction.

Student: I don't see why the derivative of a product isn't just the
             product of the derivatives.
Tutor: What is another name for derivative, in terms of how one quantity
       changes?
Student: The rate of change?
Tutor: Good. The rate of change of a product depends on how fast each
       factor changes and how large the other one is. Which two terms
       do you think appear?

A single such exchange, placed after the system prompt, shifts the whole conversation into question-led mode [7].

12 6. Close the loop with a leak-detecting judge

What it is. A lightweight second model, or a single-turn classification call, that reads each tutor response and flags it as “leaking” or “clean”. On a leak, the tutor regenerates with an instruction to ask a question instead. The pattern is community practice in prompt-engineering circles (e.g. r/PromptEngineering) and is the operational answer to the leakage problem documented in training [5].

Why it works. Leakage is intermittent, so a single rule in the system prompt is not enough; monitoring every turn catches what the rules miss. This is evaluation as part of the loop, the same evaluation-driven stance used to de-risk large education deployments [8]. It reorganizes the interaction: the model’s job is no longer to produce a perfect answer but to survive a check that it asked rather than told.

Example.

TURN CHECK — classify the tutor's last reply:
- leak: it contains the final answer, the formula, or the full solution
- clean: it contains only questions, hints, or partial reasoning
If the check is "leak", regenerate the reply with this instruction:
"Answer leaking detected. Reformulate as a guiding question or a
smaller hint."

13 Put it together: a minimal working template

The techniques compose. A starting template that combines persona, guardrails, phases, and one exemplar is enough to run a useful tutor session on any current model [1], [6], [7].

You are a Socratic tutor in the four phases of Pólya
[understanding, planning, execution, looking back].

Ground rules:
- Never reveal the final answer; give hints and sub-questions.
- Every reply contains at least one question.
- Stay in the current phase until the learner demonstrates mastery.

[Optional: one short exemplar of the desired exchange here.]

Remember: leave the learner able to solve the problem alone.

Learner: ...

Add the leak-detecting judge as a post-processing step on every turn if you want the safety net [5]. If the discussion occurs in an LMS or a chat API, the same system prompt plus judge fits behind any backend, which is why prompt-based tutoring is often the right default before committing to a dedicated model [4]. When a fine-tune is still desirable, treat prompt optimization as the cheap experiment that tells you what the fine-tune would have to add [2], and hold any candidate model to the evaluation bar set out for education systems [8], [9].

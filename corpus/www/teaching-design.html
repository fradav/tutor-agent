3  The pedagogical design

This page explains the design of this module: how you will learn and why this choice was made. Read it carefully: everything for the semester follows from it.

3.1 The general idea

Knowledge is built in interaction: with the people around you, and with the documents and tools you work with. This module puts you at the center of your learning: you design part of your own path, and you observe how you learn.

Concretely, the idea has three parts:

  1. Autonomous, co-constructed learning: you write a learning plan and follow it, with regular support.
  2. A “learning by teaching” setup: by configuring an AI tutor that must teach you correctly, you are forced to master the material. You cannot ask a machine to teach a concept you do not understand yourself.
  3. A critical approach: AI has documented limitations (hallucinations, over-reliance, superficial uses, unequal access). The course makes them an object of work and analysis.

3.2 Two tracks, identical objectives

The technical objectives are the same for everyone. Only the way of learning differs.

3.2.1 Track A: Socratic AI tutor

  • You write a structured learning plan.
  • You design a system prompt that configures a chatbot as a Socratic tutor: it does not give answers, it questions, guides, and makes you articulate your thinking.
  • You interact daily with this tutor and iterate on the system prompt based on the quality of the exchanges.
  • You keep a logbook that includes the tutor’s full conversations.

3.2.2 Track B: classic learning

  • You write the same kind of learning plan.
  • You work from the available resources: this site, the documentation, books, tutorials, last year’s course.
  • You keep a logbook of your autonomous work, and you demonstrate mastery through live-coding (board work or an individual session with the instructor).

3.2.3 Switching tracks

You can start in one track and switch to the other during the semester, as long as the choice is justified and explained in your report. The flexibility is deliberate: it lets each student find the way they learn best, and it is part of the module’s reflective work.

This is a parallel-programming course whose learning process partly relies on tools whose effects you will learn to master or work around.

3.3 Reciprocal observation

You will write a comparative report that blends two parts:

  • Reflective part: analysis of your own learning process. Which strategies worked, where difficulties appeared, how you resolved them.
  • Observational part: observation of the students in the other track. How do they learn? Does the approach seem effective, and on what criteria? What reactions does it trigger in you (envy, wariness) and why?

Track A students observe group B to understand what motivates the choice to do without AI and to justify why their own use is not mere over-reliance. Track B students observe group A to analyze the learning processes at work and to be better equipped to move through a professional world where AI is omnipresent.

Mid-semester, a cross-review session (anonymous reading of the reports and collective discussion) pools these observations. The goal is not to decide who is right, but to develop an informed relationship with technology.

3.4 Working modes

The module is designed so that each student finds a format that suits them. Every working mode counts as much as the others; no choice is interpreted as lesser commitment.

3.4.1 Solo work

  • With the AI tutor (Track A): asynchronous dialogue at your own pace, where you can rephrase, go back, and think before answering.
  • With the resources (Track B): documentation, books, tutorials, previous courses.
  • The logbook, under any circumstances, is your main channel of written expression.

3.4.2 Pair work

  • Reciprocal observation pairs work through written exchange (journals read crosswise, asynchronous comments) or orally (a 15–20 minute interview), at the participants’ choice.
  • The final project can be done solo or in pairs (communication arrangements defined by the pair itself).

3.4.3 Speaking up in group

Five channels are equivalent:

  1. Spontaneous oral speaking up;
  2. Announced oral speaking up: you signal in advance that you want to intervene;
  3. Asynchronous written contribution: written before the session (forum, shared document), read or projected by the instructor or a peer;
  4. Synchronous written contribution: written during the session (chat, collaborative document);
  5. Contribution by artifact: a diagram, code, or screenshot that carries your contribution without you having to defend it orally.

The instructor gives non-oral contributions the same weight in the discussion as oral ones.

3.4.4 Board work

“Board work” (demonstration, live-coding) is a moment with high social stakes. Four equivalent modes:

  • Classic board work;
  • Individual session with the instructor;
  • Asynchronous recording (a 5–10 minute video, projected anonymously if you wish);
  • Demonstration by artifact (commented code, architecture diagram, debugging trace).

Golden rule: no one is put in public difficulty. If you want an alternative, it is offered without justification and without penalty.

3.4.5 Signals and think time

  • The instructor leaves a think time (1–2 minutes) before asking for an answer.
  • A discreet overload signal (card, agreed gesture, message in the chat) lets you ask for a pause, and the request is respected without public comment.

The final project is assessed with the same rubric for both tracks.

3.7 The safeguards (why they are there)

  • Track A: the tutor’s full conversations in the logbook serve as objective traces. If the tutor gives a wrong answer, you must document how you detected it and corrected it. That is exactly the kind of skill the module wants to develop.
  • Track B: the proof of mastery is more direct (live-coding, individual session), and the documentation load is lighter.

3.8 AI use policy

Generative AI is explicitly allowed in this module, within a declared framework. It is a tool of the course, not a way around the work.

  1. Declare your use. In the logbook and in any graded deliverable, say where and how you used an AI. Not using AI is also fine, with no justification required.
  2. Assisted, not replaced. The university charter distinguishes guided assistance from substitution: the AI may question, guide, and challenge you; the understanding must be yours.
  3. Graded work stays with the instructor. Outside this explicit authorization, undeclared use of generative AI is academic fraud (Règlement des études, art. 2.6.4), and correction is the instructor’s alone.

This follows the Université Paul-Valéry Montpellier 3 charter on AI use.
